Somebody has to go up there.

One foot out for every four up, rails on the gutter lip and a standoff arched over onto the deck so the gutter carries no weight, three points of contact all the way. It's the least interesting part of the job and the one that ends careers.

First look is always the valley.

Water from two roof planes meets in the valley and leaves everything it carried. Moss on the shaded side because it stays wet. Bare, shiny patches on the weather side where the granules have been washed off. The sunny face two shades lighter than the rest.

None of that is visible from the driveway, and all of it tells you how many winters this roof has left.

Three tabs lifted, four nails showing.

A wind event pulls the tabs, the seal strip lets go, and the nails that held the course below are suddenly out in the rain. They rust, the rust runs, and the streak points straight at the hole.

What that looks like inside: a stain on the ceiling eight feet from here, because the water ran down the deck before it found a seam.

A roof is five layers. You can see one of them.

Pulled apart, from the rafters up. The order is not decoration — each layer only works because of the one under it.

  1. Shingles — the only layer you ever see3-tab, 5″ to weather
  2. Starter course and drip edge — the edgeat eave and rake
  3. Underlayment — the second chancesynthetic, lapped 4″
  4. Deck — what everything is nailed to7⁄16″ OSB
  5. Rafters — what carries the snow2×8, 16″ on centre

Layer thicknesses in the scene are exaggerated so the stack reads at this distance.

New courses, tucked under the old.

Cut back to sound shingles, new underlayment, then work up: every course nailed above the seal strip and under the one above it, so the fastener is never in the weather. Done right, the repair disappears within a season.
